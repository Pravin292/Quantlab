The short answer

Yes: stock prices do not move the same amount all day. The open is about twice as volatile as the middle.

This held in — of — real samples that could be tested: the open moved — to — as much as the middle.

It was found with a test (called C3) that was first proven on — simulated markets, then stress-tested under realistic conditions.

  1. Ask: is the size of price moves the same in the open, middle and close of the day?
  2. Check the test first, on simulated markets where the true answer is known.
  3. Use it once on real data, then stress-test it (Stage 6).

    26What these results do not show

    1. They do not show that C3 has a 5% error rate on real data. That is still unverified.
    2. The real samples are only partly independent: the same six stocks shaped the simulation design. The fresh US sample adds a new period and source, not new stocks.
    3. Higher volatility at the open is well known, so the rejections confirm it rather than discover it.
    4. C3 is cautious and has less power than the old test. Neither real result was close.
    5. Phase 6 ran only as an exploratory check on these same stocks. Under Option A it cannot place any sample inside the simulated domain.
    6. The stress test covers the dependence levels that were tried. It does not prove C3 is safe for stronger dependence, other markets or other stocks, and a test on stocks that played no part in the design still needs a new data pull.
    27Key terms
    H1
    Volatility is the same in the open, middle and close of the trading day.
    T
    The largest of the three period differences divided by its standard error.
    C3
    A fixed cut-off for T, chosen in advance from simulations. Above it, H1 is rejected.
    False-alarm rate
    How often a test rejects when volatility really is equal. Target 5%.
    Gated cell
    A simulated scenario with a pass limit: 5.5% (tier A) or 6.0% (tier B).
    SE
    Standard error: how uncertain an estimate is.
    Dependence
    How much stocks and days move together: persistence (a wild day is followed by another), shared volatility (stocks get wild on the same days) and return correlation.
    Power
    How often a test finds a difference that really exists.
    Placebo
    A test where the truth is equal by construction (here, labels shuffled), used to count false alarms on real data.
    Stress test
    Running the test under harder conditions than it was built for, to see where it stops being reliable.
